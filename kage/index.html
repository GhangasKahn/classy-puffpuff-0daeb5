Cuts like a fillet knife.
Marks like a chisel.
Carries like a pencil.

A sub-ounce titanium field system built around one wedge-locked handle and three Japanese blade geometries — for the angler who packs in grams, the hunter who works in seams, and the woodworker who trusts a reference face. 01 The compromise

Every ultralight knife pays for its weight somewhere you can’t see.

Below one ounce, the industry has a single recipe: thicken the geometry so it survives abuse, mold the sheath from plastic, pick the steel by price, and leave the edge to fend for itself after day three. The scale reads light. The work reads heavy. Fish skin tears instead of releasing. Layout lines wander off the knife’s belly. The edge that left home shaving arrives in camp scraping.

The alternative is worse arithmetic: carry three tools. A fillet knife for the water, a whittler for the fire, a marking knife for the bench — and a sharpener that stays home because it outweighs any of them.

The honest benchmark

Cold Steel’s Bird & Game publishes the mass class this dossier must beat: a 3.5-inch AUS-8A blade in 2.5 mm stock, polymer grip, 1.4 oz. It is a genuinely good knife, and it is named here with respect. KAGE does not try to undercut it on price or copy its geometry — it spends the same ounce differently: on titanium, powder-metallurgy steel, and an edge that gets maintained in the field.

You don’t need a lighter compromise. You need a system that refuses to compromise where it counts — and tells you plainly where it does.

02 The reframe

Treat weight as a budget, and geometry becomes the currency.

KAGE starts from a number — the grams you are willing to carry — and buys capability with geometry instead of bulk. Thin stock, hard steel and a maintained apex do more work per gram than thick stock ever can; it is the same reason serious Japanese kitchen blades are ground thin and kept hard rather than made heavy. The system spends its budget in three places:

  1. One handle, not three. A Grade 5 titanium (Ti-6Al-4V) open frame with mechanically fitted kurogaki inlays. The frame is the only handle you carry; the blades share it.
  2. Three blades, not one apology. Water, wood and fire each get a geometry ground for the job, instead of one blade pretending to do all three.
  3. A sheath that earns its grams. The saya protects the edge, indexes the sharpening angles, and converts to a power grip for heavy work. Nothing rides along for looks.

The Kusabi-Lock 楔

Kusabi is the Japanese wedge — the oldest fastener that gets tighter under load. The design intent: each blade module seats on machined registration lands in the frame’s mouth, and a hand-set titanium wedge pre-loads the joint into compression. No threads in the cutting load path. No tool required in the field. Nothing small enough to lose in the grass. The final lock mechanism is a prototype-gate deliverable — it must pass torsion, rinse-through and wet-glove testing before the design is frozen.

The flagship blade-and-handle target is 18.4 g — under half the published benchmark knife — while carrying a steel that, in published CATRA testing, matches the edge retention of K390 and Vanadis 8, and a sheath that keeps that edge honest. How the geometry earns this is the next chapter.

04 The modules

Water, wood and fire each get their own geometry.

One blade honest enough to admit its limits beats one blade that lies about them. Each module shares the same frame, the same lock, the same sheath — and refuses the jobs it wasn’t ground for.

水 MIZU-82 — the field blade

82 mm nominal edge. The flagship geometry from chapter 03: asymmetric hamaguri grind at roughly 25° inclusive, near-flat ura with the shallowest possible relief, straight reinforced heel, semi-flex distal third, low dropped point. Steel: MagnaMax, as a development candidate — released for sale May 2026, with published heat-treatment data and edge retention in the K390 / Vanadis 8 class at MagnaCut-grade corrosion resistance. Heat treatment must be validated on coupons before the window is frozen.

Ground for
Panfish to salmon, birds, caping, deer and elk seam work, pinbone tracing, camp food, everyday carry.
Not for
Long one-pass salmon fillets — a dedicated 7–9 inch fillet blade will always win that single job.

組子 KUMIKO-42 — the bench blade

42 mm nominal edge, kiridashi form. A dead-flat lapped back and a single 15° flat bevel — the back is the tool: a reference plane that registers against a square and rides wood fiber without steering. Ground in fully mirrored right- and left-hand versions, because a single bevel is handed by definition. This is the geometry Zen-Wu already builds at production grade in its shirabiki marking knife.

Ground for
Scribing against a straightedge, dovetail layout, kumiko fitting, chamfering, end-grain paring.
Not for
Prying glue lines or opening paint cans. The flat back stays flat, or it stops being a reference.

鉈 NATA-60 — the camp blade

60 mm nominal edge. MagnaCut at a 24° single bevel over a flat back — a straightforward grind chosen for repeatable, chisel-registered cuts: whittling, notching, chamfering by feel, controlled chopping strokes with the wrist, never with a baton. The flat back gives the same repeatability at the campfire that KUMIKO-42 gives at the bench.

Ground for
Feather sticks, notching, tent stakes, food prep, fitting work on the trail, a ferro rod from the dedicated square tang corner.
Not for
Batoning, prying, or bone. It says so out loud, in this dossier and in the box.

05 The materials

Zero plastic, written as a bill of materials.

Luxury in a field tool is not ornament. It is the refusal of filler — nothing in the carried system that exists because it was cheap to mold.

Excluded from the carried system, by rule: Kydex, G10, FRN, carbon-fiber composite, silicone, TPU, foam, and resin-stabilized wood. No cord wrap, no exposed adhesive, no cavity that can trap fish residue — every opening rinses through.

The case the system arrives in is packaging, not payload: washi over board, a silk blade wrap, a wool rest. It is made to be beautiful once and then left at home without guilt. The dovetail-lamination construction itself — a hard steel rail set into a titanium body — is not speculative: Zen-Wu ships it today across its production knives, at roughly the weight of a pencil.

06 Ikejime & shinkei

A quick, deliberate kill — then the geometry can keep its promise.

Ikejime (活け締め) dispatches a fish instantly with a spike to the brain, before stress chemistry can reach the muscle. It is both the humane practice and the reason the fillet later behaves the way this dossier promises. The technique and species-specific spike placement are taught well by the Ike Jime Federation; placement varies by species, and this dossier does not pretend otherwise.

The blade tip is never the spike. Driving a 0.10 mm apex through a skull is how precision edges die. A proper spike is sharp, strong, easy to grip and sized to the fish — so the KAGE TAG carries a dedicated, capped 34 mm hardened spike, and the sheath’s cross-slot turns the tag into a T-handle when it is time to use it.

KAGE SHINKEI//DUO — the spinal-wire module

  • S-50 — 0.8 mm × 500 mm nitinol wire, for backcountry trout and most bass.
  • L-80 — 1.2 mm × 800 mm, for adult Niagara-run salmon.
  • XL (optional) — 1.5 mm × 800 mm, for unusually large Chinook.
  • 64 mm vented cassette — nested channels, drain-through, no sealed cavity.
  • Dual-bore collet — the diamond sharpening tag doubles as the wet-grip wire handle.
  • Pinbone tweezer — 0.55 g beta-titanium, nested in the sheath.

The sizing is not guesswork: Japanese shape-memory shinkei wires are commercial products in exactly these diameters and lengths — Belmont lists Φ0.8–1.5 mm at up to 80 cm — with plastic grips. KAGE’s contribution is deleting the plastic: the tag you already carry becomes the handle.

For very large or thick-skulled fish, use a full-size purpose-built spike and stunning tool. A one-ounce kit does not change that, and this dossier will not claim it does.

07 The maintenance

The sheath is the sharpener. The angles are machined in, not remembered.

Field sharpening fails for one reason: freehand angles drift. So the saya carries the discipline instead of your wrist — reference facets machined at 12°, 14°, 15° and 17°, matching every working angle in chapter 03. The KAGE TAG rides in the sheath: 600-grit diamond on one face for damage, 1200-grit on the other for maintenance, a replaceable 1 μm diamond film beneath for the final polish. Dry use; rinse with water after fish.

  1. Index the tag against the facet that matches the zone you are restoring.
  2. Work the omote — light strokes, edge-trailing, until a uniform burr rises.
  3. Deburr on the film at 1 μm, alternating faces, pressure of a pencil line.
  4. Respect the ura. The reference face meets only the dead-flat plate, only flat. Lift it once and the geometry starts lying to your square.

Each step tells you it worked: the burr you can feel, the film that stops whispering, the ura that sits dead on the plate. If a stroke goes wrong, the next facet-indexed stroke corrects it — nothing here requires talent, only patience.

08 The evidence

What is known. What is being tested. What will never be claimed.

Demonstrated today

  • Pencil-weight titanium tools with dovetail-laminated MagnaCut edges are in production at Zen-Wu Toolworks — the exact construction language this dossier asks for.
  • MagnaCut’s toughness, corrosion resistance and heat treatment are published, tested metallurgy.
  • MagnaMax matches K390 / Vanadis 8 edge retention with MagnaCut-class corrosion resistance in published CATRA testing, and has been on open sale since May 2026.
  • Mission-sized shape-memory shinkei wires (Φ0.8–1.5 mm, to 80 cm) are proven commercial products.
  • The sub-1.5 oz fixed-blade class is buildable at scale — Cold Steel ships 1.4 oz today.

Targets under test

  • Every gram figure on this page, until prototypes are weighed on a scale.
  • The 62.5–63.0 HRC window, verified per blade with retained coupons.
  • Tip flex of 2.5–3.5 mm under a 10 N lateral load.
  • Behind-edge targets of 0.10–0.16 mm across the zones.
  • Reference-face flatness and land coplanarity within 0.02–0.03 mm.
  • The Kusabi-Lock joint under torsion, grit, and wet-glove abuse.
  • Saltwater exposure, sanitation rinse-through, and wood-scribing accuracy — the prototype acceptance gates.

Never claimed

  • No batoning, prying, pelvis splitting, chopping antler or heavy bone, or using the point as a screwdriver. A saw or axe handles heavy wood.
  • No pretense that an 82 mm blade equals a dedicated 7–9 inch salmon fillet knife on that one job.
  • No price, no availability, no “limited stock.” Quotes do not exist yet.
  • No Zen-Wu endorsement. This is a proposal to Zen-Wu, and it says so everywhere.
  • No testimonials, no awards, no review scores. There is no product to review.

The pilot path

  1. Four engineering samples — two right-hand, two left-hand — built to the Rev A targets.
  2. The acceptance gates — bench, wet-field, corrosion, sharpening and sanitation testing against chapter 08. Failures revise the drawing, not the marketing.
  3. A 50-unit pilot — provisionally 40 right-hand, 10 left-hand — contingent on prototype acceptance.
  4. Quotes before commitments — pricing compared at 25, 50 and 100 units before anything is binding.

Two decisions close this dossier

The dominant hand of the first user, and the pilot quantity the collaboration can honestly commit to. Everything else is drawn, budgeted, and waiting for steel.
