# Japanese Joinery (Kigumi) & Construction Standards

## Philosophy
Traditional Japanese timber joinery prioritizes mechanical interlocking connections that remain visible, allow controlled wood movement, and require no glue for structural integrity. Strength comes from precise geometry, tight shoulders, and the interaction of multiple contact surfaces rather than adhesive or hidden metal.

## Preferred Joint Hierarchy for Structural Work
1. Housed or through mortise-and-tenon with drawbored peg (most common for rails and beams).
2. Keyed or wedged through-tenons.
3. Complex interlocking joints (ari, koshikake, etc.) when the design demands higher resistance to separation or rotation.
4. Mechanical fasteners only as secondary reinforcement or for modern hybrid elements.

Glue is acceptable only for non-structural or interior decorative work.

## Drawbored Pegged Tenon (Standard for Rails)
- Tenon thickness typically 1/3 to 1/2 of rail thickness depending on load.
- Housing (haunch or full housing) recommended for exterior work to protect the joint from weather and improve shoulder bearing.
- Peg diameter commonly 1/2" or scaled to member size.
- Drawbore offset usually 1/16" (1.5 mm) toward the shoulder for most softwood and hardwood applications; adjust only with clear justification.
- Pegs should be riven when possible for strength and driven after dry fit and verification.

## Layout Discipline
- Japanese carpentry traditionally works from centerlines. When a package adopts Japanese character, prefer centerline layout for posts, beams, and major rails.
- Establish a clear reference centerline for each member and transfer all joint geometry from that line.
- Use story poles, ink lines, and precise gauges. Document the transfer method on the joint sheet.

## Cutting Sequence Principles
- Knife the shoulders first to create a clean wall.
- Cut the tenon cheeks carefully, staying slightly proud if needed, then pare to final.
- Mortises are chopped or bored and cleaned with chisels; provide drain relief at the bottom/back of exterior mortises.
- Always dry-fit, check shoulder contact under raking light, then transfer the peg hole from the post to the tenon with the correct offset.

## Wood Movement & Climate Adaptation
- Design joints so seasonal movement does not open critical shoulders or create water traps.
- Prefer elevated primary members and clear drainage paths.
- For Western New York or similar freeze-thaw climates, prioritize end-grain sealing, pitched tops, and joints that shed water rather than relying solely on traditional Japanese open detailing.

## Aesthetic Integration
- Visible pegs, clean shoulders, and consistent rhythm are part of the design language.
- When using Japanese joint names, always pair them with precise modern dimensional callouts so the builder is never dependent on external knowledge of the traditional form.

## Integration with Planforge Packages
- On any J-sheet using Japanese methods, state both the traditional intent and the exact geometry.
- Declare the layout system (centerline preferred) on G and A sheets.
- Include 3D-print prototyping recommendations for complex interlocking joints.
